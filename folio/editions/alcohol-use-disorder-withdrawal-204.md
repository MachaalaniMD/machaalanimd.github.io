# Alcohol Use Disorder & Withdrawal
> Give thiamine before glucose, start CIWA, and treat withdrawal early. Benzodiazepines are first line; phenobarbital for complicated or refractory withdrawal. Before discharge, offer naltrexone or acamprosate. Treat use disorder like any chronic disease.

## Withdrawal: timeline
Symptoms start hours to days after stopping or cutting down heavy, prolonged drinking.
| Syndrome | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild–moderate** (CIWA 10–18) | Within 48 h | Tremor, sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, headache, anxiety; oriented |
| **Withdrawal seizures** | 6–48 h | Usually one GTC seizure or a short burst. Treat immediately with lorazepam or diazepam to prevent more. Status epilepticus → look for another cause |
| **Alcoholic hallucinosis** | 12–48 h | Visual/tactile > auditory hallucinations; patient knows they aren't real; clear sensorium |
| **Delirium tremens** | 72 h (3 days), up to 2 weeks | Tremor, sweats, tachycardia, hypertension, fever, disorientation, inattention, hallucinations the patient believes; agitation; CIWA usually >20 |

**Severe-withdrawal predictors:** prior DTs or withdrawal seizures, comorbidities (especially TBI), age >65, hypertension, low Na or K, platelets <150. {JAMA 2018;320:825} PAWSS predicts severity on admission.

## Withdrawal: treat
:::key Everyone
- **Thiamine** IV/IM (before dextrose), folate 1 mg daily, multivitamin
- **D5-LR** for alcoholic ketoacidosis; replete electrolytes (especially phosphate and K)
- **CIWA-Ar** q1–4h; stop once <8 for 24 h (or 6 consecutive scores <8)
- Offer an **addiction consult** for use-disorder treatment
:::
| CIWA-Ar | Severity | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **<10** | Mild | Outpatient supportive care is fine: non-caffeinated fluids, multivitamin, thiamine 100 mg PO for 3–5 days, return precautions. {ASAM 2020} Or a taper, gabapentin preferred over benzodiazepines {Alcohol Clin Exp Res 2009;33:9}: 1200 mg load + 600 mg q6h on day 1 (or 1200 mg/day for days 1–3), tapering to 300–600 mg/day by days 4–7 |
| **10–18** | Mild–moderate | Benzodiazepines (phenobarbital if benzodiazepines are contraindicated or there's a history of complicated withdrawal). Carbamazepine or gabapentin can be added |
| **>19** | Severe | **Always treat; can be fatal.** IV benzodiazepine or phenobarbital |

### Benzodiazepine or phenobarbital?
| | Benzodiazepine | Phenobarbital |
|---|---|---|
| **Use when** | First line for mild–moderate withdrawal without complicated withdrawal in the last 2–3 years | DTs, seizures or ICU stay for withdrawal in the last 2–3 years; patient preference; resistant or refractory withdrawal (CIWA not improving despite high benzodiazepine doses) |
| **Dosing** | **Oral over IV** when possible; no agent is clearly more effective. **Symptom-triggered** by CIWA if the patient can communicate (don't treat purely subjective symptoms; use mMINDS if they can't). Scheduled dosing generally not recommended | **Load by ideal body weight:** 6–10 mg/kg in 3 divided doses (6–8 mg/kg if sedation is high-risk). **Rescue:** 2 mg/kg for CIWA >15 with coarse tremor and SBP >165, HR >115 or diaphoresis (not for agitation or hallucinations alone). IV on the floor needs pulse oximetry and telemetry |
| **Choosing / notes** | Lorazepam: shorter-acting, preferred in liver disease and older adults. Diazepam, chlordiazepoxide: longer half-life, smoother course, but more oversedation in susceptible patients. Track CIWA, which symptoms are being scored, and the daily total | Auto-tapers (half-life 1–4 days), predictable, wide therapeutic window. GABA-ergic and anti-glutamate |
| **Avoid / watch** | Acute angle-closure glaucoma, AMS/delirium, active DTs not responding. **Resistance:** >6 mg lorazepam/h. **Toxicity** can mimic DTs. Switch to phenobarbital if CIWA isn't improving | History of SJS/TEN or acute intermittent porphyria; unstable breathing. Relative: likely self-directed discharge, recent completed treatment (<10 days). Apnea, hypoventilation, bradycardia, hypotension, laryngospasm. **CYP3A4 inducer** (lowers apixaban and many others) |
| **Afterward** | Consult addiction or psychiatry if planning a scheduled taper | **Drinking after an IV/IM load can be fatal** (more sensitive to alcohol and benzodiazepines). Refractory → addiction/psychiatry input; oral taper possible in high-risk ICU patients |

**Adjuncts (never alone):** clonidine for autonomic hyperactivity and anxiety; β-blockers for persistent hypertension or tachycardia in select patients; haloperidol for agitation or hallucinations (may lower the seizure threshold).

### Evaluate
- **History:** time of last drink; past severe withdrawal (seizures, DTs, hallucinosis, ICU or intubation); co-ingestions (including benzodiazepines); recent changes in use; suicidal ideation and psychiatric illness
- **Labs:** CMP, CBC, LFTs, tox screen, serum ethanol; serum osm if HCO3 <15 or anion gap acidosis; CK if found down (>5000 = rhabdo); pregnancy test if applicable (OB if third trimester, preterm risk or fetal alcohol concern)
- **Ethanol clears ~15–20 mg/dL/h** (15–35 range; faster with chronic use). {J Forensic Sci 1993;38:104}

| Blood alcohol (mg/dL) | Typical effect (varies with tolerance) |
|---|---|
| <80 | Legal driving limit |
| 100 | Impaired judgment |
| 200 | Confusion, staggering |
| 300 | Stupor |
| 400 | Coma, death |

**Why withdrawal happens:** chronic alcohol boosts GABA tone, so glutamate up-regulates. Stopping suddenly leaves glutamate unopposed → noradrenergic surge and more dopamine.

## Wernicke–Korsakoff
:::warn Wernicke encephalopathy: treat on suspicion
- **Caine criteria** (≥2, 85% sensitive): dietary deficiency, eye-movement abnormality, cerebellar signs (leg ataxia), AMS or poor memory. A serum B1 level doesn't diagnose it. {J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry 1997;62:51}
- **Treat:** thiamine 500 mg IV TID × 7 days (IV > IM; infuse over 30 min), then 100 mg daily while at risk. Doses vary, but untreated Wernicke can become irreversible Korsakoff.
- **Prevent** in anyone at risk: 200 mg IV × 1 day, or 200 mg PO BID × 4 days, then 100 mg daily at discharge.
:::
**Korsakoff (chronic):** anterograde + retrograde amnesia with confabulation, apathy, intact sensorium.

## Alcohol use disorder
- **1 standard drink = 14 g ethanol** (5 oz wine, 12 oz beer, 1.5 oz spirits). Quantify use to counsel on risk.
- **Risky use** (not a diagnostic criterion): any drinking in pregnancy; binge ≥5 drinks per occasion (men) or ≥4 (women); heavy ≥15/week (men) or ≥8/week (women), or binging on ≥5 days in the past month. {NIAAA}
- **Chronic effects:** cytopenias; low K, Mg, Ca, phosphate, vitamin D; alcoholic ketoacidosis (low gluconeogenesis → relative hypoglycemia → low insulin → ketosis); lactic acidosis (high NADH/NAD) {NEJM 2017;377:1368}; gastritis, ulcers, pancreatitis, liver injury and cirrhosis.
- Malnutrition is common: check electrolytes; daily multivitamin with folate and thiamine. {Drug Alcohol Depend 2017;179:229}

### Medications for moderate–severe AUD {JAMA 2018;320:815}
| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Naltrexone** (first line) | PO 50 mg daily (12.5–25 mg if side effects are a concern), up to 50–100 mg; or IM 380 mg every 4 weeks | Likely better than acamprosate. {JAMA 2006;295:2003} **Screen for opioid use first:** blocks opioids and precipitates withdrawal. Not with acute hepatitis/liver failure, current opioids, buprenorphine or methadone. Nausea, fatigue, low appetite; LFTs every 6 months |
| **Acamprosate** (first line) | 666 mg TID; 333 mg TID if CrCl <50 | Contraindicated if CrCl <30 or pregnant. Fine with liver disease |
| **Disulfiram** (second line) | 125–250 mg daily, then 250–500 mg/day | Only for people already abstinent who want to stay that way, not to cut down. Reaction with alcohol: nausea, flushing, vomiting, sweating, hypotension, palpitations, rarely serious CV events. Avoid with psychosis or severe heart disease |
| **Off-label** (if the above fail) | Topiramate 100–150 mg BID; gabapentin ~600 mg TID {Addiction 2019;114:1547}; baclofen 5 → 15 mg TID {Am J Psychiatry 2018;175:86} | |

- **Success** ranges from fewer heavy-drinking days to abstinence. Use harm reduction and set goals together.
- **Psychosocial:** 12-step programs raise abstinence rates more than other counseling approaches. {Cochrane Rev 2020}

## Substance use: the basics
### Screen everyone
- **Alcohol:** AUDIT-C for unhealthy use; CAGE for dependence. {USPSTF}
- **Drugs:** "How many times in the past year have you used a recreational drug or a prescription medication for nonmedical reasons?" {Arch Intern Med 2010}

### History
Most recent use, amount, frequency, route, triggers · benefits vs harms; medical, psychiatric and behavioral complications; harm-reduction practices · age of first use, family history, periods of abstinence · past treatment (meds, programs, mutual support) · the patient's current goal (cut down or stop).

### Diagnosis (DSM-5-TR)
Use isn't the same as a use disorder. Count criteria over 12 months: **2–3 mild, 4–5 moderate, ≥6 severe.** Remission: early (>3 months but <1 year), sustained (>1 year).
1. Taken in larger amounts or for longer than intended
2. Unsuccessful efforts to cut down
3. Much time spent getting, using or recovering
4. Craving
5. Failure to meet obligations at work, school or home
6. Continued use despite social or interpersonal problems
7. Activities given up
8. Use in physically hazardous situations
9. Continued use despite physical or psychological harm
10. Tolerance
11. Withdrawal, or using to avoid it

- Ask which substance the patient sees as the biggest problem.
- Avoid labels like "polysubstance use disorder": stigmatizing, and not a DSM diagnosis.
- **Tox screens don't diagnose a use disorder** (false positives and negatives). Use them to check for other substances, monitor opioid treatment and open a conversation.

### Approach
- Person-first language and trauma-informed care. People with substance use disorders face bias from clinicians.
- **Prevent self-directed discharge** by treating pain, agitation and discomfort. {Subst Abus 2020;41:519} Involve the addiction team early; think carefully before involving security (e.g. have them on standby rather than at the bedside).
- **Treatment:** medications (many can be prescribed by a PCP), counseling (motivational interviewing, CBT), peer support (recovery coaches, AA, SMART Recovery).

### Levels of care after discharge
Usually: hospital or medically supervised withdrawal → stabilization and transitional programs → long-term residential treatment → sober living. Outpatient options are **partial hospitalization** (6–8 h/day, 5 days/week) and **intensive outpatient** (~3 h/day, 3 days/week, for 1–3 months). Dual-diagnosis inpatient units treat a substance use disorder with a primary psychiatric illness and safety concerns.
