# Atrial Fibrillation & Flutter
> Three decisions for every patient: control the rate (or shock if unstable), decide on anticoagulation with CHA₂DS₂-VASc, and choose rate vs rhythm control. Early rhythm control now has an edge. {AHA/ACC/HRS, Circ 2024;149:e1-e156}

## AF with RVR: right now
::: Unstable (SBP <80) | Usually HR >150 with shock, refractory pulmonary edema or angina
- **Synchronized cardioversion,** start at 150 J biphasic (see *ACLS: Tachycardia*)
- If pressors are needed, **phenylephrine** first (its reflex bradycardia helps)
- HR >140 alone can cause hypotension. Lower rates cause it when there's systolic or diastolic dysfunction or low preload (lost atrial kick).
:::
::: Peri-stable (SBP 80–90) | Borderline BP
- Try **low-dose β-blocker or diltiazem** carefully (with IV fluid if pulmonary edema isn't a concern)
- **BP-sparing options:**
  - **Amiodarone** 150 mg IV over 10 min, repeat ×1 if needed, then 1 mg/min. It may cardiovert, so weigh this if not anticoagulated.
  - **Digoxin** IV 8–12 mcg/kg ideal body weight in 3 doses (50%, then 25% and 25% q4–8h), max 1.5 mg in 24 h. Level 6–24 h after the load (goal <1.2). Adjust for eGFR; watch interactions. Contraindicated with an accessory pathway.
:::
::: Stable (SBP >90) | Rate control
Use **IV if HR >130 or symptomatic,** then switch to oral once controlled.
- **Magnesium** 2–4 g IV empirically: improves rate and rhythm control with nodal agents {J Cardiol 2021;78:375}
- **Metoprolol** (preferred in most): IV 2.5–5 mg over 2 min q5 min PRN, max 15 mg; oral tartrate 6.25–50 mg q6h. Caution with severe bronchospasm or rapid escalation in decompensated HF.
- **Diltiazem:** IV 0.25 mg/kg (usually 10–25 mg) over 2 min, repeat q10–15 min PRN; oral up to 360 mg/day total. Reduce for liver or kidney impairment. **Avoid if LVEF <40% or decompensated HF.**
:::
**Always fix the trigger** once stable: infection, volume, PE, alcohol, pneumothorax, pneumonia, MI, pericarditis.

:::warn No AV nodal blockers if there's pre-excitation on the ECG
Use IV procainamide. Be cautious with high-grade AV block.
:::

## Cardioversion
- **Urgent:** ischemia, organ hypoperfusion, symptomatic hypotension, severe pulmonary edema. **Elective:** new AF or intolerable symptoms from persistent AF.
- **Electrical:** synchronized, start at 150 J biphasic and increase if needed (see *ACLS: Defibrillation, Cardioversion & Pacing*).
- **Chemical:** works much better for AF <7 days old.

:::key Anticoagulation around cardioversion (electrical or chemical)
- **Before:** onset clearly <48 h → may proceed without anticoagulation. Onset >48 h or unknown → anticoagulate 3 weeks first, **or** TEE/CT to exclude LA thrombus right before.
- **After:** anticoagulate at least 4 weeks (atrial stunning, recurrence). Beyond that, decide by CHA₂DS₂-VASc and HAS-BLED.
- Any interruption in anticoagulation during the prior 3 weeks means high stroke risk.
:::

## Stroke prevention
### CHA₂DS₂-VASc
1 point each: **C**HF, **H**TN, **A**ge 65–74, **D**iabetes, female **S**ex, **V**ascular disease. 2 points: **A**ge ≥75, **S**troke/TIA. {Thromb Haemost 2012;107:1172}
| Score | Anticoagulate? |
|---|---|
| 0 | No |
| 1 (2 in women) | Clinical judgment; usually lean toward yes |
| ≥2 (≥3 in women) | Yes |

- Not used for HOCM or moderate-or-worse mitral stenosis (anticoagulate those).
- Scores don't capture every risk; individualize. AF burden matters. {JAMA Card 2018;601} Subclinical AF raises stroke risk too; anticoagulation there trades fewer strokes for more major bleeding. {NEJM 2012;366:120}

### HAS-BLED (bleeding risk)
HTN (SBP >160), abnormal kidney (CrCl <50) or liver function (cirrhosis, bilirubin 2× or AST/ALT/ALP 3× ULN), stroke, bleeding history, labile INR (<60% time in range), elderly (>65), drugs (aspirin, NSAIDs) or alcohol (>8 drinks/week). **≥3 = caution and close follow-up,** not a reason to withhold. The SPARC tool helps weigh options.

### Which anticoagulant {Stroke 2010;41:2731}
- **DOACs over warfarin** for everyone except mechanical valves, moderate–severe mitral stenosis, HOCM and valvular AF. Warfarin may beat rivaroxaban in rheumatic heart disease. {NEJM 2022;387:978}
- DOACs lower stroke, death and ICH but raise GI bleeding {Lancet 2014;383:955}; apixaban has the lowest GI bleeding risk. {Ann Intern Med 2022;175:1515}
- **Apixaban 2.5 mg BID** if 2 of 3: Cr ≥1.5, weight ≤60 kg, age ≥80. Other doses: *Anticoagulation Agents*.
- **CrCl <15 or dialysis:** warfarin or apixaban.
- Bridging: *Anticoagulation Management*.

### Left atrial appendage occlusion
The appendage is the source of ≥90% of thrombi in AF-related stroke.
- **Watchman:** stroke prevention comparable to warfarin, with less bleeding and better survival in non-valvular AF. {JACC 2017;70:2964} Consider if long-term anticoagulation isn't possible.
- **Surgical occlusion** during cardiac surgery if CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥2, plus continued anticoagulation (Class 1A).

## Rate vs rhythm control
- Older trials found them equivalent (AFFIRM, RACE, PIAF, STAF, HOT CAFÉ, AF-CHF). **Current guidelines favor early rhythm control** and lowering AF burden.
- In AF diagnosed within 1 year plus cardiovascular disease, rhythm control (drugs or ablation) lowered CV death, stroke and HF/ACS admissions (EAST-AFNET 4).
- **Favor rhythm control:** symptoms affecting quality of life, age <65, heart failure (especially reduced EF). NSR can improve quality of life and exercise capacity. {NEJM 2005;352:1861; JACC 2004;43:241}

### Rate control
- **β-blockers beat diltiazem** (70% vs 54% controlled), alone or with digoxin.
- **Digoxin** alone controls resting rate moderately (onset 3–6 h) and fails with high adrenergic tone. Long-term use is linked to higher mortality. {JACC 2018;71:1063}
- **Target:** lenient (resting HR <110) is as good as strict (<80) (RACE II). Stricter control (or rhythm control) may help younger patients or those with HF.
- **No diltiazem/verapamil if LVEF <40%.**

### Rhythm control {Circ 2012;125:381}
| Heart | Drug options |
|---|---|
| **No structural disease** | Flecainide, propafenone, dofetilide, dronedarone, sotalol, amiodarone |
| **CAD** | Dofetilide, dronedarone, sotalol, amiodarone |
| **HF or LVH** | Amiodarone, dofetilide |

- **Pill-in-the-pocket:** for infrequent, well-tolerated paroxysms, take flecainide or propafenone (+ β-blocker or diltiazem) at onset. {NEJM 2004;351:2384}
- **Catheter ablation (PVI):** increasingly first line {NEJM 2021;384:305}. Fewer recurrences than drugs in paroxysmal (MANTRA-PAF) and persistent AF {EHJ 2014;35:501}. Lowers morbidity and mortality in HF (CASTLE-AF) and improves distress. {JAMA 2023;330:925}
- **AV node ablation + pacemaker** when rate and rhythm control both fail. {JACC 2014;64:2246}

## New AF: work-up
- **History:** timing of symptoms; HTN, diabetes, valve disease, HF, angina, congenital heart disease, OSA, family history; triggers (alcohol, thyrotoxicosis, stimulants, surgery, MI, myocarditis, PE, lung disease, infection)
- **ECG:** no discernible P waves, irregularly irregular R–R. A regular rhythm in AF suggests an escape rhythm and complete heart block.
- **TTE:** LV function, atrial size, valves, pulmonary pressures (TEE or CT for LA thrombus)
- **CXR** and **labs:** TSH, LFTs, BUN/Cr, CBC, NT-proBNP, BMP with K and Mg
- Longer monitoring (Holter, Zio) if needed; stress test if ischemic symptoms
- A smartwatch AF alert has PPV 0.84 on a simultaneous ECG in people >65. {NEJM 2019;381:1909-1917}

**Risk factors:** age, obesity, HTN, smoking, alcohol, diabetes, prior MI, HF, OSA. AF often recurs with a secondary trigger (surgery, infection, MI, thyrotoxicosis, binge drinking, PE). Higher incidence in white and male patients {JACC 2020;76:2982}; inequities in screening and treatment persist. {Lancet Reg Health Eur 2024;37:100786}

| Stage | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 · At risk | Modifiable or non-modifiable risk factors |
| 2 · Pre-AF | Structural or electrical substrate (atrial enlargement, ectopy) |
| 3 · AF | 3A paroxysmal (<7 days), 3B persistent (>7 days), 3C long-standing persistent (>12 months), 3D after successful ablation |
| 4 · Permanent | Rhythm control no longer pursued |

## Atrial flutter
- **ECG:** sawtooth flutter waves, atrial rate 250–300 with 2:1 conduction (ventricular rate ~150); can be variable, 3:1 or 4:1. **1:1 conduction** can briefly precede VT/VF.
  - **Typical (type 1):** right atrial loop through the cavotricuspid isthmus. Counterclockwise (common): inverted flutter waves in II, III, aVF, upright in V1. Clockwise: the reverse.
  - **Atypical (type 2):** often faster and harder to ablate.
- **Anticoagulate as for AF** (embolic risk is lower). {J Stroke Cerebrovasc 2018;27:839; Chest 2012;141:e531S}
- **Rate control** uses the same drugs but is harder to achieve.
- **Rhythm control: ablation beats drugs.** CTI ablation for typical flutter is >90% effective at 1 year. {Circ Arrhythm EP 2009;2:393} Prophylactic CTI ablation during PVI doesn't help those without known flutter. {Arrhythm EP Rev 2022;e10}
