# Potassium Disorders
> Hyperkalemia: get a STAT ECG; if it's abnormal, give calcium first; shift K into cells; then remove it. Hypokalemia: replete Mg too, prefer oral KCl, and avoid dextrose. {NEJM 2015;373:60}

## Hyperkalemia: act now
:::warn Treat if any of these
ECG changes, K >6, a rapidly rising K, or symptoms. Acute rises are the most dangerous. **Never give amiodarone if hyperkalemia is possible:** sine waves can look like VT, and amiodarone can be fatal here.
:::
| Step | Drug and dose | Onset | Lasts | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Stabilize** | **Calcium** gluconate or CaCl2 (central line) 1–2 g IV; repeat q5 min PRN | 1–3 min | 30–60 min | First line for any ECG change; stabilizes the cardiac membrane. Avoid with digoxin |
| **2. Shift** | **Insulin** 5 U IV + D50 25 g (if glucose <250) | 10–30 min | 4–6 h | Lowers K by 0.5–1.5 mEq/L |
| | **Albuterol** 10–20 mg nebulized | 15–30 min | 15–90 min | Avoid with active ACS or significant tachyarrhythmia |
| | **Bicarbonate** 1–2 amps IV, or drip | 5–10 min | 1–2 h | Best when pH is very low |
| **3. Remove** | **Furosemide** ≥40 mg IV (rough dose: 40 × Cr if K >6); give with NS if volume is needed; add a thiazide if very overloaded | 30 min | Variable | Urinary K loss |
| | **Lokelma** (sodium zirconium) 10 g TID > **patiromer** 8.4 g daily PRN | 1 h; 7 h | 24 h | Trades K for Na or Ca in the gut. Avoid binders with obstruction, constipation or ileus |
| | **Hemodialysis** | Immediate | 3 h | Removes K; may rebound as K shifts back out |

- Removal is what fixes it; shifting only buys time. Also fix volume status, start a low-K diet, and stop offending meds.
- Kayexalate is largely replaced by Lokelma (efficacy concerns, GI complications). {JAMA Intern Med 2019;179:1023}

## Hyperkalemia: confirm and explain
- **Symptoms:** cramps, ascending weakness, paralysis, conduction delay (complete heart block, bundle branch block, sinus arrest), arrhythmia (VT/VF, asystole, idioventricular rhythm) {CCM 2008;36:3246}
- **Rule out pseudohyperkalemia:** hemolyzed sample, platelets >500K, WBC >120K (the lab flags red-cell hemolysis, not white-cell lysis), or blood drawn near a K-containing infusion. A blood gas K helps.
- **ECG progression:** peaked T waves and shorter QT → longer PR ± AV block, flat P waves → wide QRS ± bundle branch block → sine wave → PEA/asystole/VF. The ECG does not track K level reliably. {Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2008;3:324}

### Causes
Persistent hyperkalemia almost always means the kidney isn't excreting K.
- **Shift out of cells** (usually transient unless the kidney is impaired): cell lysis (hemolysis, rhabdo, tumor lysis), acidosis, low insulin, hyperosmolality, digoxin, β-blockers, minoxidil, hyperkalemic periodic paralysis, rewarming after hypothermia
- **Less aldosterone effect:** ACEi/ARBs, NSAIDs, K-sparing diuretics, calcineurin inhibitors, pentamidine, trimethoprim, type IV RTA
- **Less Na reaching the distal nephron:** hypovolemia, heart failure, cirrhosis
- **AKI/CKD,** especially oliguric; usually GFR <15
- **Other:** ureterojejunostomy (urine K is reabsorbed)

Mnemonic: **A**cidosis, low **A**ldosterone; **B**eta-blockers, **B**lood; **C**ell lysis; **D**rugs, **D**M, **D**ecreased GFR.

## Hypokalemia
- **Symptoms,** usually when K <3: cramps, ileus, ascending weakness (can reach respiratory muscles), rhabdo, paralysis, worse hepatic encephalopathy {Nat Rev Neph 2010;7:75}
- **ECG:** flat T waves, ST depression, U waves, long QT, ectopy → VT/VF (especially if K <2.5, on digoxin, or otherwise susceptible)

### Replete
- Each **10 mEq raises K ~0.1**. Be careful with high Cr, or when the cause is a shift (e.g. periodic paralysis).
- **Oral KCl first** (ER pill or IR powder): safer, quick, and most patients are Cl-depleted too
- **IV KCl** if oral isn't possible: max 10 mEq/h on the floor, 20 mEq/h in the ICU
- **Always replete Mg,** or K keeps leaking out through ROMK. {JASN 2007;18:2649}
- **Avoid dextrose-containing fluids:** insulin release drives K into cells and makes it worse

### Causes
- **Lab artifact:** WBC >100K takes up K if the sample sits (check a blood gas K)
- **Low intake:** rarely the only cause
- **Shift into cells:** alkalemia, insulin, refeeding, β-agonists (albuterol, epinephrine), rapid cell production (after G-CSF), hypothermia, toxins (cesium, barium, chloroquine), antipsychotics, hypokalemic or thyrotoxic periodic paralysis
- **GI losses:** diarrhea (chronic, VIPoma, villous adenoma, laxatives), vomiting, NG suction
- **Kidney losses, normal BP:**
  - High urine flow (primary polydipsia, lots of IV fluid), low Mg, drugs (amphotericin B, ifosfamide, cisplatin, gentamicin)
  - With acidemia: DKA, RTA (proximal type II, some distal type I)
  - With alkalemia: diuretics, upper GI losses (secondary hyperaldosteronism), Bartter (like a loop diuretic), Gitelman (like a thiazide)
  - Unabsorbed anions in the urine: β-hydroxybutyrate (DKA), bicarbonate (vomiting), toluene, penicillin metabolites
- **Kidney losses, high BP:**
  - Primary hyperaldosteronism (high aldosterone, low renin; e.g. adrenal adenoma)
  - Secondary (both high; renal artery stenosis, renin-secreting tumor)
  - High cortisol or ENaC activity: Cushing, Liddle, black licorice

## How the body handles K
- Absorbed K is taken up by liver and muscle through insulin and β2 receptors (Na-K ATPase). 98% of K is inside cells.
- Rising extracellular K triggers aldosterone, which makes principal cells secrete K into the urine.
- More Na delivered to the distal nephron means more K excreted; less Na means K retained.
