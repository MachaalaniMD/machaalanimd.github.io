# DKA & HHS
> Order of priorities: airway and volume, then potassium, then glucose. Never start insulin with K <3.3. Close the anion gap, not just the glucose. {NEJM 2015;372:546}

## Diagnose
| | DKA | HHS |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | 250–800 | >600 (often >1000) |
| pH / HCO3 | pH <7.3, HCO3 <18 | pH >7.3 |
| Anion gap | >10 | Usually normal |
| Ketones | Present (serum β-hydroxybutyrate) | Absent or minimal |
| Osmolality | Variable | >320 |
| Onset | Hours to days | Days to weeks |
| Mental status | AMS possible | AMS in 25–50%; obtundation, seizures, focal deficits |
| Fluid deficit | ~5–8 L | ~8–10 L |

- **Euglycemic DKA:** consider with SGLT2 inhibitors, alcoholic liver disease, pregnancy.
- **Presentation (DKA):** dehydration, polyuria/polydipsia, weight loss, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, AMS, Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath.
- **Labs:** BMP, CBC with diff, UA, serum osm, serum β-hydroxybutyrate, VBG/ABG (if bicarbonate low or hypoxemic). Consider troponin, ECG, blood and urine cultures, CXR, lipase.
- **Urine ketones miss β-hydroxybutyrate,** the main ketone in DKA. Measure it in serum.
- **Sodium:** use the measured Na for the anion gap. Use corrected Na to look for true hypotonic hyponatremia: corrected Na = measured Na + 0.02 × (glucose − 100).

:::tip Find the trigger: the "I"s
Infection (30–40%, often pneumonia or UTI) · Initial presentation of diabetes (20–25%) · Insulin non-adherence · Inflammation (pancreatitis; amylase/lipase can rise in DKA without it) · Infarction (MI, stroke, gut) · Intoxication (alcohol, cocaine) · Iatrogenic (SGLT2 inhibitors, steroids, thiazides, dobutamine/terbutaline, atypical antipsychotics) · Infant (pregnancy)
:::

## Treat
**Monitor:** BMP q2h until the gap closes, then q4h until K is normal. VBG and β-hydroxybutyrate q2–4h. Fingerstick glucose q1h on an insulin drip.

### 1. Fluids {QJM 2012;105:334; JAMA Netw Open 2020;3:e2024596}
- **LR 15–20 mL/kg/h for the first 1–2 h** (less with heart failure, ESLD, ESRD or hypoxemia)
- Then by corrected Na: **low** → NS or LR ± K at 250–500 mL/h. **Normal/high, or hyperchloremic acidosis** → ½NS or LR ± K at 250–500 mL/h
- **Add D5 at 150–200 mL/h** once glucose is <250 (DKA) or <300 (HHS), and keep the insulin running

### 2. Potassium
Total-body K is low (200–400 mEq deficit) even if serum K looks normal or high. Insulin will drop it further.
| Serum K | Do |
|---|---|
| **<3.3** | KCl 20–40 mEq IV per hour. **Hold insulin until K >3.3** |
| **3.3–5.3** | Add 20 mEq K to each liter of fluid |
| **>5.3** | No K; recheck q2h |

### 3. Insulin
:::note Standard regimen
Regular insulin drip 0.1 U/kg/h (with or without a 0.1 U/kg bolus), or 0.14 U/kg/h without a bolus. Aim for glucose to fall ~50–75 mg/dL per hour. Keep the drip going until the gap closes, adding dextrose as glucose falls. Glucose goal while on the drip: 150–200 in DKA, 250–300 in HHS. {ADA/EASD/JBDS consensus, Diabetes Care 2024;47:1257}
:::

### Other electrolytes
- **Bicarbonate:** no benefit if pH >6.9. If pH <6.9: 2 amps in 400 mL sterile water with 20 mEq KCl over 2 h.
- **Phosphate:** low total-body stores, but serum level may look normal or high and will fall with insulin. Replete only if <1.0, to avoid cardiac dysfunction.

## Switch to subcutaneous insulin
:::key Ready when all three are true (DKA)
1. Glucose <200
2. Anion gap <12
3. HCO3 ≥15 or pH >7.3
:::
For HHS: glucose <300, mental status improved, and able to eat.
- **Basal,** one of:
  - Home glargine dose
  - Glargine 0.25–0.4 U/kg/day
  - Glargine = (units on the drip in the last 6 h) × 4 × 0.7
  - New diabetes: consider NPH 0.25–0.4 U/kg/day, ⅔ in the AM and ⅓ in the PM (easier to titrate)
- **Overlap the drip and the first basal dose by 2 hours.** Stopping the drip before basal insulin acts brings ketones and glucose right back.
- **Mealtime:** 0.25–0.4 U/kg/day divided (type 1 or unknown), or sliding scale only (type 2)
- Keep dextrose fluids until the patient eats at least ~50% of meals (D5W, or D10W if NPO).

## Special situations
- **Ketosis-prone diabetes:** DKA in someone with type 2, or atypical for type 1 (older, overweight). Discharge on insulin and refer to endocrinology for antibodies (GAD65, IA2) and C-peptide. Long-term insulin may not be needed.
- **HHS:** insulin is low but present, so ketones are suppressed. Mortality is much higher than in DKA. {Diab Care 2014;37:3124} People with type 2 and a burnt-out pancreas can also get DKA.

## Why it happens
- **Diabetes:** low insulin plus high counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol) → hyperglycemia → osmotic diuresis → hypovolemia
- **Ketones:** low insulin → lipolysis → free fatty acids → ketones (acetoacetate, β-hydroxybutyrate, acetone)
- **Acidosis:** ketoacids, plus contraction alkalosis on top of a total-body bicarbonate deficit
